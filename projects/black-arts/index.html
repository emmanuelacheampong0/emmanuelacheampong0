Behind the work

My artwork is a digital collage inspired by the Black Arts Movement and my understanding that Black art can be both creative expression and a form of resistance, cultural pride, community, and self-definition. I explored different visual references on Pinterest and used Photoshop to layer, recolor, arrange, and combine them into one composition. At the center, I placed a Black woman surrounded by musicians, artists, raised fists, crowds, migration imagery, African patterns, and urban spaces because I wanted Black identity to appear as something connected to many histories, cultures, and experiences rather than a single story. This reflects the Black Arts Movement’s belief that art could become a collective force for Black liberation and empowerment.

The words “Our People, Our Stories, Our Art, Our Power” capture the main message of my piece: when Black people have the freedom to tell their own stories and represent themselves, art itself becomes a form of power. Similarly, “Resist, Create, Build, Heal, Belong” shows that resistance is not limited to protest; it can also mean preserving culture, creating spaces for Black voices, healing communities, and building belonging. Music appears throughout the collage because Black music has carried culture, memory, and resistance across generations, including through the Great Migration and later movements such as hip-hop. I also incorporated Ghanaian patterns and Adinkra symbolism to bring my own Ghanaian perspective into the work. Adinkra is an Akan visual tradition in which symbols communicate values, ideas, proverbs, and cultural knowledge, making art itself a way of preserving memory. This connects to BAM artists drawing from Black and African traditions and rejecting Eurocentric standards of artistic value. Finally, “Past, Present, Future” represents the continuity of Black creative expression. Ultimately, I wanted my collage to show that Black art can remember the past, resist the present, and imagine a future in which Black people continue to have the power to tell their own stories.

“Black art can be more than something beautiful; it can also be a way of resisting oppression, preserving memory, building community, and giving Black people the power to define themselves.”

Emmanuel Sefah Acheampong
