Our People.

A digital exhibition exploring Black artistic expression, cultural memory, and resistance through a Ghanaian lens.

This project brings the Black Arts Movement into conversation with African visual traditions I grew up around. It asks how a symbol, an image, and a story can carry memory—and help people see themselves as makers of culture.

01 / The idea

Art can remember. Art can refuse.

The Black Arts Movement used creative work to assert Black identity, challenge exclusion, and imagine new forms of community power. This project treats that history as a living conversation: visual culture can preserve stories, question who gets represented, and invite people to make meaning for themselves.

As a Ghanaian student, I brought Adinkra into the visual language. These symbols connect the work to Akan and wider Ghanaian traditions of communicating ideas through image. Their presence is a bridge between cultural memory and the movement’s insistence that Black people create, name, and shape their own stories.

02 / A Ghanaian visual vocabulary

Symbols with room to think.

The exhibition references symbols whose meanings deepen its themes. They are not decoration: each one invites a different way to think about history, strength, identity, and change.

SankofaReturn and learn

Often represented by a bird looking back, Sankofa expresses the value of returning to the past to retrieve what can guide the future. It connects cultural memory to the act of reclaiming overlooked histories.

Gye NyameExcept for God

A widely recognized symbol expressing the supremacy and uniqueness of God. Its presence locates the artwork within a Ghanaian visual tradition where belief, identity, and everyday life are closely woven.

DwennimmenStrength with humility

Ram’s horns suggest strength and humility together. That pairing offers a way to read resilience as both powerful and grounded in community.

NkyinkyimInitiative and change

Its twisting form evokes life’s changing path, initiative, and adaptability. It resonates with artistic movements that transform constraint into new expression.

Meanings can vary across makers and contexts. These short descriptions are interpretive entry points; the symbols deserve to be encountered within their cultural histories and the artwork itself.
03 / Making the work

From visual research to digital exhibition.

Building this project brought together HTML, visual design, Black history, identity, and cultural symbolism. I had to think about more than what an image says: how a visitor enters, what they notice first, and what context helps them look again.

The project is an experiment in digital storytelling and design—how layout, sequence, and cultural references can shape a visitor’s encounter with history. It also helped me see code as a space for curation: a way to organize, frame, and make a story explorable.
